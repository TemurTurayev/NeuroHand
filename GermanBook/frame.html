    Klang: Phonetics & Sound Rules

    German is 100% phonetic. Learn 6 key sound shifts and you can read any German medical textbook out loud perfectly.

    Unlike English, German has strictly consistent spelling rules. Once you know how combinations like ei, ie, sch, st, sp, and ch sound, you will never mispronounce a word.

    The Golden Sound Rules
    • ei = "ai" (like my): nein, Freund
    • ie = "ee" (long e): wie, Sie, Bremen
    • st / sp at start of word = "sht / shp": Spital (shpital), Strasse (shtrasse)
    • W = English "V": Wasser (vasser), wer (ver)
    • V = English "F": Vater (fater), viel (feel)
    • Z = "ts": Arzt (artst), Zeit (tsait)
    • Ä, Ö, Ü = Umlauts (Softened vowels: ä=e, ö=ø, ü=y)
    ei = [aɪ] (ein)   vs   ie = [i:] (viele)
    How do you pronounce the 'W' in German words like Wasser or Wer?

    Artikel: The Three Genders (der, die, das)

    German nouns are always capitalized and always have a gender. Here is how to master der, die, and das with color rules and suffix shortcuts.

    Never memorize a German noun alone. Always learn it with its article: der (masculine, blue), die (feminine, red), das (neuter, green).

    Suffix Cheat Code (Gender Predictors)
    • ALWAYS DIE (Feminine): -ung (die Wohnung), -heit (die Gesundheit), -keit (die Möglichkeit), -schaft (die Wissenschaft), -ion (die Injektion).
    • ALWAYS DAS (Neuter): -chen (das Mädchen), -lein, -ment (das Medikament), -um (das Zentrum).
    • ALWAYS DER (Masculine): -ling (der Schmetterling), -ismus (der Organismus), -or (der Doktor).
    What is the gender of 'Gesundheit' (health) based on its suffix '-heit'?
    Kapitel 3 Recap
    • You know the 3 gender articles: der, die, das.
    • You master the suffix predictors: -ung/-heit/-keit (die), -chen/-ment/-um (das).

    V2: Golden Word Order Rule

    The Golden Rule of German Syntax: In a main clause, the conjugated verb ALWAYS sits at Position 2.

    In English, you can say "Today I go to Bremen". In German, if you put "Heute" (Today) at Position 1, the verb MUST immediately follow at Position 2: "Heute gehe ich nach Bremen."

    [Position 1: Time / Topic] + VERB (Pos 2) + [Subject] + [Rest]
    Example Transformation
    Normal: Ich arbeite heute im Hospital.
    Inverted (Focusing on 'Today'): Heute arbeite ich im Hospital.
    Inverted (Focusing on 'In Hospital'): Im Hospital arbeite ich heute.

    Kapitel 4 Recap
    • Strict V2 law: The conjugated verb never moves from Position 2 in standard statements.

    Akkusativ: Direct Objects & Movement

    The Accusative case (Винительный падеж / Tushum kelishigi). Good news: only masculine (der → den) changes!

    When something receives the direct action of a verb (I see a doctor, I drink coffee), it goes into the Accusative case. In German, only masculine articles change from der/ein to den/einen!

    GenderNominativ (Subject)Akkusativ (Direct Object)
    Masculineder / ein Mannden / einen Mann
    Femininedie / eine Fraudie / eine Frau
    Neuterdas / ein Kinddas / ein Kind
    Pluraldie / keine Bücherdie / keine Bücher
    Akkusativ Prepositions: DOGFU

    These prepositions ALWAYS trigger Akkusativ: durch (through), ohne (without), gegen (against), für (for), um (around).

    Fill in the blank: "Ich brauche ___ Arzt." (I need a doctor [der Arzt - masculine])
    Kapitel 5 Recap
    • Masculine shift: der → den, ein → einen, kein → keinen.
    • Feminine, Neuter, and Plural remain identical to Nominativ.

    Nicht vs. Kein: German Negation

    How to say 'no' or 'not'. The crisp rule for choosing between kein (no/not any noun) and nicht (not verbs/adjectives).

    English uses 'no' and 'not'. German uses kein (to negate nouns with indefinite or zero articles) and nicht (to negate verbs, adjectives, proper names, or specific nouns with 'der/die/das').

    The Decision Matrix
    • Use KEIN: Nouns with ein/eine or no article. (Ich habe keinen Hunger. Ich trinke keinen Kaffee.)
    • Use NICHT: Verbs, adjectives, adverbs, proper names, or nouns with der/die/das. (Ich verstehe nicht. Das ist nicht gut.)
    Which word negates: "Ich habe ___ Zeit." (I have no time [die Zeit])
    Kapitel 6 Recap
    • Kein = negates nouns (declinable like ein).
    • Nicht = negates verbs, adjectives, and specific terms.
    U1 · Nomen & Fälle · Kapitel 7

    Plural: Patterns of German Plurals

    All plural nouns take 'die' in Nominativ. Discover the 5 main plural ending patterns.

    In English, you just add '-s'. In German, plural formation depends on word origins and suffixes. All plural nouns use the article die in Nominativ!

    The 5 Plural Patterns
    1. -(e)n: Most feminine nouns (die Frau → die Frauen, die Nation → die Nationen).
    2. -e (± Umlaut): Many masculine/neuter nouns (der Tag → die Tage, der Arzt → die Ärzte).
    3. -er (± Umlaut): Many short neuter nouns (das Buch → die Bücher, das Kind → die Kinder).
    4. -s: Foreign loanwords & abbreviations (das Auto → die Autos, das EKG → die EKGs).
    5. ∅ (No ending ± Umlaut): Nouns ending in -el, -en, -er (der Apfel → die Äpfel, der Computer → die Computer).
    What is the plural of 'das Symptom' (foreign/latin origin)?
    Kapitel 7 Recap
    • Plural article is always die.
    • 5 main ending patterns: -(e)n, -e, -er, -s, ∅.

    Modale: Modal Verbs & The Sentence Bracket

    Können, müssen, wollen, sollen, dürfen, mögen. Master modal verb conjugation and the verb bracket (Satzklammer).

    Modal verbs express ability, necessity, permission, or desire. They introduce a Sentence Bracket: the modal verb takes Position 2, while the main action verb goes all the way to the END in Infinitive form!

    Subject + MODAL (Pos 2) + [Objects / Details] + INFINITIVE (End)
    In der Klinik (At the Clinic)
    Ich muss den Patienten heute untersuchen.
    I must the patient today examine.
    I must examine the patient today.
    Können Sie Deutsch sprechen?
    Can You German speak?
    Can you speak German?
    Which sentence places the infinitive correctly at the end?
    Kapitel 8 Recap
    • Modal verb at Position 2, Infinitive main verb pushed to the end.
    U2 · Verben & Zeit · Kapitel 9

    Präfixe: Separable & Inseparable Verbs

    Trennbare Verben: Why German prefixes pop off and land at the end of the sentence.

    German verbs can take prefixes that change their meaning. Some prefixes are separable (trennbar) and split off in present tense, flying to the end of the sentence!

    Separable vs. Inseparable
    • Separable (Stressed prefix): an-, ab-, aus-, mit-, ein-, vor-, zu-. (ankommen: Der Zug kommt um 8 Uhr an.)
    • Inseparable (Unstressed: be-ge-er-ver-zer): besuchen, verstehen, erklären. (Ich verstehe den Satz. No separation!)
    Conjugate 'anrufen' (to call by phone - separable): "Ich ___ dich später ___."
    Kapitel 9 Recap
    • Separable prefixes (an, aus, mit, ein) land at sentence end.
    • Be-ge-er-ver-zer never separate.

    Dativ: The Indirect Object Case

    Dative Case (Дательный падеж / Jo'nalish kelishigi). The case of location, recipient, and the famous prepositions.

    Dative answers "Wem?" (To whom?) or "Wo?" (Where at rest?). In Dative, ALL articles change: der/das → dem, die → der, plural die → den (+n on noun)!

    GenderNominativDativ (Indirect Object / Location)
    Masculineder / ein Manndem / einem Mann
    Femininedie / eine Frauder / einer Frau
    Neuterdas / ein Kinddem / einem Kind
    Pluraldie / keine Kinderden / keinen Kindern
    Always Dative Prepositions: aus-bei-mit-nach-seit-von-zu

    Mnemonic tune: Aus, bei, mit, nach, seit, von, zu — always take Dativ with you!
    Example: Ich fahre mit dem Bus nach der Arbeit.

    Fill in: "Ich helfe ___ Arzt." (I help the doctor [der Arzt - masculine])

    • Dative transformations: der/das → dem, die → der, Plural die → den (+n).
    • Prepositions: aus, bei, mit, nach, seit, von, zu.

    Perfekt: Spoken Past Tense

    How Germans talk about yesterday. Auxiliary (haben/sein) at Pos 2 + Partizip II at the end.

    In spoken German, 90% of past actions are expressed using Perfekt. You build it with an auxiliary verb (haben or sein) at Position 2 and the Partizip II (ge-mach-t, ge-komm-en) at the end.

    Subject + haben / sein (Pos 2) + [Details] + Partizip II (ge-...-t / -en)
    When to use SEIN instead of HABEN?

    Use SEIN as auxiliary if the verb expresses movement from A to B (gehen, kommen, fahren, fliegen) or a change of state (einschlafen, aufstehen) or is sein/werden/bleiben.
    For all other verbs (action without positional shift), use HABEN.

    Complete: "Ich ___ gestern nach Bremen gefahren." (I drove to Bremen yesterday)
    Kapitel 11 Recap
    • Perfekt = haben/sein at Pos 2 + Partizip II at sentence end.
    • Sein for movement & change of state.

    Nebensatz: Subordinate Clauses (weil, dass, wenn)

    The Uzbek verb order phenomenon in German! Conjunctions like weil/dass kick the conjugated verb to the absolute end.

    In Uzbek syntax, verbs naturally come at the end of clauses (...chunki men nemis tilini o'rganyapman). German subordinate conjunctions (weil = because, dass = that, wenn = if/when) do exact same thing: they send the conjugated verb to the VERY END!

    Main Clause, + weil / dass / wenn + Subject + [Details] + CONJUGATED VERB (END)
    Example

    Ich lerne Deutsch, weil ich in Deutschland arbeiten möchte.
    (I learn German because I want to work in Germany.)

    Which sentence structure is correct for a subordinate clause with 'weil'?
    Kapitel 12 Recap
    • Subordinate conjunctions (weil, dass, wenn, ob) force verb-final position.

    Adjektive: Adjective Endings Demystified

    Why do adjectives change endings? Weak, strong, and mixed declensions explained with the 'Signal Law'.

    If an adjective comes right before a noun (a good doctor), it takes an ending. The rule is simple: Someone must carry the gender signal. If the article (der/die/das) carries the signal, the adjective takes a simple '-e' or '-en'. If there is no article, the adjective carries the signal!

    Weak Declension (After Definite Articles: der/die/das)
    • Nominative Singular: -e (der gute Arzt, die gute Frau, das gute Kind)
    • All Dative & Plural forms: -en (mit dem guten Arzt, die guten Ärzte)
    Select the correct ending: "Das ist ein ___ Tag." (gut - der Tag [masculine nom.])
    Kapitel 13 Recap
    • Signal principle: Either article or adjective must carry gender markers (-r, -e, -s).

    Höflich: Politeness & Konjunktiv II

    How to sound respectful in German hospitals and everyday life: "Könnten Sie...", "Ich hätte gern...", "Würden Sie...".

    In Germany, medical settings and formal situations require polite structures. Instead of demanding "Geben Sie mir Wasser!", learn the magic Konjunktiv II forms.

    Höflichkeitsformeln (Polite Phrases)
    Ich hätte gern einen Kaffee, bitte.
    I would have gladly a coffee, please.
    I would like to have a coffee, please.
    Könnten Sie mir bitte helfen?
    Could You to me please help?
    Could you please help me?
    Würden Sie bitte das Fenster öffnen?
    Would You please the window open?
    Would you please open the window?
    What is the polite way to ask for a glass of water in a clinic?

    • Konjunktiv II keys: hätte gern (would like), könnte (could), würde + Inf. (would).
